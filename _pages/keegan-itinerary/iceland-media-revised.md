    <h2>From Iceland, Around the World</h2>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">
      Iceland has played a surprisingly large role in modern prosthetics. Despite having a population smaller than many American cities, it is home to Össur, one of the largest prosthetic manufacturers in the world. The company was founded in 1971 by Icelandic prosthetist and amputee Össur Kristinsson, whose work with silicone liners helped establish the company before it expanded into prosthetic knees, feet, and other components.
    </p>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">
      I came to Reykjavík interested in what happens farther upstream from the clinics and fabrication facilities I had already seen. How does an engineer designing a prosthetic knee learn what users need? And how much of that design is really an engineering problem, as opposed to a healthcare, manufacturing, cultural, or economic one? My visit to Össur's headquarters brought me to the root of those questions almost immediately.
    </p>

      <div class="itinerary-story__opening-copy">
        <h2>From the Clinic Back to the Engineer</h2>
        <p>
          I met with a product design engineer in Össur's R&amp;D department who has worked extensively on Navii, the company's magnetorheological microprocessor knee (MPK). His mechanical-engineering work has focused particularly on the actuator: the part of the knee that produces and controls resistance as the joint bends.
        </p>
        <p>
          Before the visit, I had been thinking about engineers and prosthetic users as opposite ends of a chain. A user experiences the device every day, while an engineer in Reykjavík may be designing one tiny part inside it. I wanted to know how information travels back up that chain. When something feels wrong on a hill in Germany, for instance, how does that observation ever reach the people writing firmware or redesigning the next knee?
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
        <h2>Who Gets a Say in the Design?</h2>
        <p>
          One answer is the prosthetist. The engineer described clinician feedback as an important link between users and R&amp;D. A prosthetist sees how a component behaves across many people and can report patterns that an individual user might never frame as an engineering problem. He gave me an example from Germany, where clinician feedback contributed to a firmware change affecting how Navii behaves while walking uphill. I liked this example because nothing physical had to be replaced: the same knee could behave differently after its software changed.
        </p>
        <p>
          Össur also works with highly experienced users who put products through unusually demanding real-world conditions. That kind of testing answers a different question from a laboratory test. A machine can repeat a load thousands of times; a person can reveal the strange, messy combinations of movement, terrain, weather, habits, and mistakes that happen in actual life. For a product already on the market, much of the engineer's work therefore becomes iterative: learn where the current design can be more durable or useful, then carry those lessons into updates and future products.
        </p>
        <p>
          It made me reconsider what “user-centered design” can mean for a mature medical device. It is not necessarily an engineer sitting beside every future user while sketching a product from scratch. Sometimes it is a much longer feedback loop: user to clinician, clinician to manufacturer, field testing back to engineering, and engineering back out into another version of the device.
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
        <h2>When Newer Manufacturing Isn't Automatically Better</h2>
        <p>
          I also asked about additive manufacturing. I keep encountering young upper-limb companies built around 3D printing, so I was curious whether a large manufacturer like Össur was moving in the same direction. The answer was more restrained than I expected. In R&amp;D, 3D printing is extremely useful for prototypes, fit checks, and quickly testing geometry. But for many high-volume commercial components, established processes such as molding and machining can still be faster, cheaper, and easier to control at scale.
        </p>
        <p>
          That distinction has started to follow me from place to place. A technology can be exciting without being the best manufacturing method for every part. The more useful question is not “Will 3D printing replace traditional fabrication?” but “Where does it actually solve a problem better?”
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
        <h2>Designing Inside a Healthcare System</h2>
        <p>
          The part of our conversation that surprised me most was how far healthcare policy reaches upstream. In the United States, prosthetic components are commonly billed through codes and payer rules that help determine whether a device will be covered. I was used to thinking about those constraints at the clinic: a prosthetist may know what they want to prescribe, only to discover that the payer will not authorize it. I had thought much less about the engineer in Iceland who has to design products for that same market.
        </p>
        <p>
          We discussed the coexistence of Navii and the RHEO KNEE as one example of a broader problem: a newer product does not automatically make an older one irrelevant. Different devices can occupy different clinical, functional, price, and reimbursement positions. Keeping multiple options available can matter when a user is a better match for one device than another—or when a payer will fund one and not the other. I left wanting to understand this interaction more carefully, because it means the product portfolio itself can partly reflect the structure of the healthcare systems buying from it.
        </p>
        <p>
          The engineer was also familiar with a more uncomfortable issue I had heard about in the United States: reimbursement can create financial incentives around component selection. Different products and configurations may be reimbursed differently, creating the possibility that business incentives and the best clinical choice do not always point in exactly the same direction. That does not mean clinicians generally prescribe for reimbursement rather than need, but the possibility of a conflict is important. What surprised me was hearing the issue discussed from the manufacturing side. I still do not know what meaningful responsibility a manufacturer has—or what leverage it really possesses—when the incentive originates farther downstream. That is a question I want to keep following.
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
        <h2>A Prosthesis You Want People to See—or Need to Hide</h2>
        <p>
          Then our conversation moved from reimbursement to something much more visible: appearance. The engineer described growing interest in making prostheses intentionally noticeable rather than disguising them. Össur's Earth Echoes covers for Navii, for example, turn a protective shell into a choice of color inspired by Icelandic landscapes. The idea is simple, but I find the implication interesting. A component does not have to pretend to be a biological leg. It can look engineered, personal, even decorative.
        </p>
        <p>
          Almost immediately, though, he gave me a counterexample. He recounted a user's description of prosthetic legs being stolen in South Africa because of their value, creating an incentive to conceal an expensive device rather than display it. I have not independently verified how widespread that experience is, so I do not want to generalize it to a country or region. But the contrast stuck with me.
        </p>
        <p>
          The same visible technology can signal pride and self-expression in one context and unwanted attention or risk in another. That complicates the language of “stigma” that I had been using in my own head. Whether somebody displays a prosthesis is not just a question of confidence. Economics, safety, culture, and geography can all shape the decision.
        </p>
        <p>
          It also changed one assumption behind this project. I had imagined seeking out MPK users in demanding environments to ask how sophisticated devices survive heavy wear. But in places where an expensive prosthesis attracts the wrong kind of attention, simply finding someone comfortable discussing or displaying it may be harder than I expected. The conditions surrounding a prosthesis shape not only how it is used, but how easy it is for an outsider like me to study that use.
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
        <h2>Who Should Have Access?</h2>
        <p>
          One of our last conversations before heading upstairs was about Russia and Ukraine. Össur suspended sales to Russia on February 24, 2022 following Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. That fact led us into a question I had not expected to be thinking about during a prosthetics visit: what obligations does a medical-technology company have during a war?
        </p>
        <p>
          Prostheses are commercial products, but mobility does not feel like an ordinary commercial good. Withdrawing from a market can be a form of economic pressure; it can also reduce access for ordinary people who have no control over their government's actions. Continuing to sell medical devices can preserve that access; it can also raise questions about what it means for a company to continue doing business in an aggressor state. I do not have a clean answer, and I am not sure there should be one. What interested me was realizing that even a decision about where to sell a knee can become a decision about ethics, access, and geopolitics.
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
        <h2>Inside the Knee</h2>
        <p>
          Upstairs, the conversation became physical. The engineer handed me parts that normally disappear inside a finished prosthesis, including the compact stack at the heart of Össur's magnetorheological knee technology. Navii is a microprocessor knee, but unlike MPKs that regulate a conventional hydraulic system, it uses magnetorheological (MR) fluid to vary resistance.
        </p>
        <p>
          MR fluid contains tiny magnetizable particles suspended in a carrier fluid. Inside the rotary actuator, the fluid occupies very small gaps between alternating metal rotor and stator elements. As the knee rotates, those elements shear the fluid. Applying a magnetic field changes the fluid's resistance to that shear, allowing the actuator to produce more or less braking torque. The important point is not that the fluid literally becomes a solid; it is that its resistance can change rapidly and controllably. Sensors and software determine how much resistance the knee should provide as the user moves.
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <p class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
      Seeing the actuator in someone's hand did more for my understanding than looking at a finished knee ever could. From the outside, an MPK is a clean black enclosure. Inside is a system of bearings, electronics, magnetic circuitry, fluid, and extraordinarily tight mechanical tolerances. Suddenly the words <em>verification</em> and <em>testing</em> felt less like manufacturing jargon and more like the obvious consequence of asking this small assembly to control a person's knee thousands of times a day.
    </p>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
        <h2>Not Every “Bionic” Component Does the Same Thing</h2>
        <p>
          The lab also gave me a useful comparison between passive control and active power. Navii controls resistance at the knee; it does not drive the user's leg forward with a motor. The Power Knee, by contrast, is a motor-powered MPK that can actively assist movements such as walking, standing up, and negotiating ramps or stairs. The distinction sounds technical, but it changes what the device can do—and what it demands in weight, batteries, control, and engineering.
        </p>
        <p>
          The PROPRIO FOOT approaches the problem from the ankle. It is a microprocessor-controlled prosthetic foot with motorized ankle movement. Among other functions, it can dorsiflex during swing to increase toe clearance and can adjust ankle position for changing terrain and different heel heights. In everyday terms, it is trying to solve problems that a static prosthetic foot cannot solve by itself: catching a toe, meeting a slope at the wrong angle, or changing shoes and suddenly changing the alignment of the entire leg.
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
        <h2>From R&amp;D to the Manufacturing Floor</h2>
        <p>
          The last part of the visit brought the scale of the operation back into view. We moved from components small enough to hold between two fingers into a large manufacturing space filled with machining, cutting, pressing, assembly, and inspection equipment. Individual parts move through processes such as kitting, actuator assembly and filling, pressing, final assembly, verification, and testing before a finished device ever leaves the facility.
        </p>
        <p>
          I had come to Össur expecting the most interesting part to be the advanced technology. Instead, I kept noticing the infrastructure required to make advanced technology boringly dependable. A clever actuator matters; so does filling it consistently, assembling it correctly, checking it, and being able to repeat that process at scale. Innovation gets the attention. Reliability is what somebody has to live on.
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
        <h2>What Changed for Me in Reykjavík</h2>
        <p>
          In Tennessee, I had started to see a prosthesis as the product of a network rather than a single clinician or workshop. Össur pushed that network farther upstream. The engineer is connected to the prosthetist; the prosthetist to the user; the product manager to reimbursement systems; the manufacturing floor to the design; and all of them to the social and economic environment in which the device will eventually be worn.
        </p>
        <p>
          That makes the word <em>device</em> feel increasingly incomplete. A knee can be mechanically brilliant and still be inaccessible. It can be approved by a payer but poorly matched to a user. It can be durable in a test rig but difficult to live with. It can be designed to stand out in Reykjavík and deliberately hidden somewhere else. The engineering matters enormously. What I am starting to understand is how many non-engineering decisions become embedded in the engineering before the product ever reaches a person's body.
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

  <section class="itinerary-story__section">
    <div class="itinerary-story__section-label">
      <span>01</span>
      <p>FabCo · Aug. 3, 2026</p>
    </div>
    <div class="itinerary-story__opening-copy">
      <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">Inside the socket workshop</p>
      <h2>The device begins long before the finished prosthesis.</h2>
      <p class="itinerary-story__lead">
        Inside FabCo Prosthetic Designs in Nashville, the work moved between
        models, ovens, vacuum lines, resin, cutting tools, sanding belts, and
        repeated inspection. Each station translated clinical instructions
        into an object a person may wear against the body for most of the day.
      </p>
      <p>
        FabCo is a prosthetics-only central fabrication facility that produces
        custom work for clinics around the country. The shop handles much more
        than lower-limb sockets: upper-limb devices and specialty jobs arrive
        with different geometries, components, and performance demands. What
        unites them is the need to turn a digital file, cast, or modified model
        into a device that is strong, repeatable, and specific to one person.
      </p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <div class="itinerary-story__perspectives">
    <article>
      <i class="fas fa-drafting-compass" aria-hidden="true"></i>
      <p class="itinerary-story__card-number">The geometry</p>
      <h3>Shape carries clinical intent.</h3>
      <p>
        Contours, reliefs, and trim lines encode where the socket should
        support the body, where it should create space, and how it should move.
      </p>
    </article>
    <article>
      <i class="fas fa-layer-group" aria-hidden="true"></i>
      <p class="itinerary-story__card-number">The material</p>
      <h3>Strength is arranged, not added.</h3>
      <p>
        Thermoplastics, resins, carbon reinforcement, and flexible materials
        are selected and placed according to the device’s purpose.
      </p>
    </article>
    <article>
      <i class="fas fa-hand-paper" aria-hidden="true"></i>
      <p class="itinerary-story__card-number">The interface</p>
      <h3>Millimeters become lived experience.</h3>
      <p>
        An edge, surface defect, or small change in fit can influence comfort,
        confidence, maintenance, and how long the device can be worn.
      </p>
    </article>
  </div>

      <span>02</span>
      <p>Following the process</p>
    </div>
    <div>
      <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">From instructions to interface</p>
      <h2>A repeatable sequence with room for judgment.</h2>
      <p class="itinerary-story__lead">
        No single workflow describes every prosthesis. Materials and steps
        change with the prescription, the limb level, the component system,
        and the needs of the user. Lower-limb fabrication nevertheless follows
        a recognizable progression that makes careful handoffs possible.
      </p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <div class="itinerary-story__detail">
    <div>
      <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">Variation by design</p>
      <h3>Upper- and lower-limb work do not follow the same script.</h3>
    </div>
    <p>
      High-volume lower-limb work often moves through established material and
      fabrication sequences. Upper-limb and specialty devices can vary much
      more widely because suspension, control method, component placement,
      range of motion, and the intended task all change the problem. The shop’s
      process is therefore less a fixed recipe than a framework for making
      different designs reliably.
    </p>
  </div>

  <section class="itinerary-story__section">
    <div class="itinerary-story__section-label">
      <span>04</span>
      <p>Technology &amp; judgment</p>
    </div>
    <div>
      <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">What changes—and what holds</p>
      <h2>A new tool matters only when the result is dependable.</h2>
      <p class="itinerary-story__lead">
        FabCo has added faster digital carving and scanning that can preserve
        socket geometry as an STL file. Those tools improve speed and
        documentation without removing the need for inspection and finishing.
      </p>
      <p class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
        A 3D printer intended for test sockets offered a useful contrast. In the
        shop’s experience, inconsistent results kept it from becoming part of
        routine production. The lesson was not that newer methods lack value,
        but that fabrication technology earns trust through repeatability. A
        process that affects fit must work reliably on an ordinary production
        day—not only in an ideal demonstration.
      </p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="itinerary-story__section">
    <div class="itinerary-story__section-label">
      <span>05</span>
      <p>The wider network</p>
    </div>
    <div>
      <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">No workshop stands alone</p>
      <h2>The prosthetics community functions through overlapping relationships.</h2>
      <p class="itinerary-story__lead">
        The facility felt less like an isolated vendor than one node in a
        national network. The team knew the clinicians I had previously
        shadowed, fabricated for adaptive-sport work, exchanged knowledge with
        other specialists, and discussed projects involving companies such as
        WillowWood and Fillauer.
      </p>
      <p>
        FabCo also contributes fabrication insight to research and development
        and shares work at meetings of the American Orthotic and Prosthetic
        Association and the American Academy of Orthotists and Prosthetists.
        Those relationships matter because a prosthesis crosses several kinds
        of expertise before it reaches the person who will use it: clinical
        evaluation, design, fabrication, component engineering, fitting, and
        long-term adjustment.
      </p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <div class="itinerary-story__detail">
    <div>
      <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">Why the network matters</p>
      <h3>Daily experience sends information back upstream.</h3>
    </div>
    <p>
      A user experiences the completed system, not the organizational lines
      between clinic, manufacturer, and fabrication shop. Comfort problems,
      component failures, activity goals, and cosmetic preferences all create
      feedback. The field advances when that information can travel back to the
      people who prescribe, design, and make the device.
    </p>
  </div>

  <section class="itinerary-story__section">
    <div class="itinerary-story__section-label">
      <span>06</span>
      <p>Designed for daily life</p>
    </div>
    <div>
      <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">What this visit clarified</p>
      <h2>The most technical decisions are often the least visible.</h2>
      <p class="itinerary-story__lead">
        A finished socket can look simple beside a microprocessor knee, powered
        hand, or other recognizable component. The visit made clear that its
        unseen geometry and workmanship help determine whether the entire
        system can become an ordinary part of someone’s day.
      </p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <div class="itinerary-story__findings">
    <article>
      <span>01</span>
      <h3>The socket is a human interface.</h3>
      <p>
        It transfers forces while protecting living tissue, so manufacturing
        quality is inseparable from comfort, mobility, and confidence.
      </p>
    </article>
    <article>
      <span>02</span>
      <h3>Reliability shapes access.</h3>
      <p>
        A repeatable process reduces rework and delays, helping clinics return a
        usable device to the person who needs it.
      </p>
    </article>
    <article>
      <span>03</span>
      <h3>Innovation is collaborative.</h3>
      <p>
        New materials and tools become meaningful when fabricators, clinicians,
        engineers, and users can compare results and improve them together.
      </p>
    </article>
  </div>

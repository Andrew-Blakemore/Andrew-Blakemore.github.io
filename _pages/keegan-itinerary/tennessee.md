  <section class="itinerary-story__section itinerary-story__section--opening">
    <div class="itinerary-story__section-label">
      <span>01</span>
      <p>The visit</p>
    </div>
    <div class="itinerary-story__opening-copy">
      <p class="itinerary-story__lead">
        A prosthetic foot, knee, or hand may attract the most attention, but the
        socket is the part that meets the body. It transfers force, supports
        weight, and remains against a person's skin for hours at a time.
      </p>
      <p>
        FabCo is a prosthetics-only central fabrication facility affiliated with
        Amputee Associates. Instead of operating like a local clinic, it works
        as an extension of prosthetists' practices: clinics provide the design
        information, and FabCo's technicians turn that information into custom
        sockets. The company says it fabricates thousands of sockets each year
        for clinics across the United States.
      </p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="itinerary-story__section" id="fabrication">
    <div class="itinerary-story__section-label">
      <span>02</span>
      <p>Fabrication</p>
    </div>
    <div>
      <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">From prescription to finished socket</p>
      <h2>One shape, translated through many steps</h2>
      <p class="itinerary-story__lead">
        The process is a sequence of handoffs. Each step preserves—or subtly
        changes—the geometry established by the clinician.
      </p>
    </div>
  </section>

      <figcaption>Trimlines are marked before material is removed. Their position affects both support and freedom of movement.</figcaption>

    <div class="itinerary-story__feature-notes">
      <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">A simplified process</p>
      <ul class="itinerary-story__checklist">
        <li>
          <strong>Model</strong>
          <span>A cast or digital file becomes a positive form of the residual limb.</span>
        </li>
        <li>
          <strong>Shape</strong>
          <span>The model is modified to create relief where tissue needs space and support where it can carry load.</span>
        </li>
        <li>
          <strong>Form</strong>
          <span>Thermoplastic may be heated and drawn over the model, or reinforcing fibers may be laminated with resin.</span>
        </li>
        <li>
          <strong>Finish</strong>
          <span>The socket is released, cut to its trimlines, sanded, inspected, and prepared for assembly.</span>
        </li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="itinerary-story__quote">
    <blockquote>“It was baptism by fire.”</blockquote>
    <p>Nick Gambill · describing his early training in fabrication</p>
  </div>

  <p class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
    Nick described prosthetic fabrication as a craft advanced through trial,
    error, and unusually open communication. Technicians around the country
    call him with difficult cases, and he shares what has worked—and what has
    not. That exchange is not incidental. In a field this specialized, shared
    practical knowledge is part of the infrastructure.
  </p>

  <aside class="itinerary-story__detail">
    <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">Why lower and upper limbs differ</p>
    <h3>Volume makes repetition possible.</h3>
    <p>
      Lower-limb sockets still require individual designs, but common loading
      patterns and a larger case volume support a more repeatable workflow.
      Upper-limb devices vary more widely by amputation level, suspension,
      control strategy, terminal device, and the tasks a person wants to
      perform. The result is a less uniform fabrication path and more case-by-case problem-solving.
    </p>
  </aside>

      <ul class="itinerary-story__checklist">
        <li>
          <strong>Carving</strong>
          <span>A faster carver shortens model production without changing the underlying design logic.</span>
        </li>
        <li>
          <strong>Scanning</strong>
          <span>Finished shapes can be converted to STL files, creating a digital record for documentation or reproduction.</span>
        </li>
        <li>
          <strong>Printing</strong>
          <span>A 3D printer was tested for check sockets, but at this facility the results were not consistent enough for routine use.</span>
        </li>
        <li>
          <strong>Standard</strong>
          <span>Whatever the tool, the output must be dependable enough to place against a person's body.</span>
        </li>
      </ul>

  <aside class="itinerary-story__detail">
    <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">A comparison to carry forward</p>
    <h3>Europe is a question, not a conclusion.</h3>
    <p>
      We discussed European use of prepreg composites and different production
      conventions. The European Union does regulate custom-made medical devices
      through requirements for safety, performance, risk management, and
      documentation, but I could not verify a rule requiring the “best possible”
      material. Comparing those real-world choices with European fabricators is
      now a question for the fellowship rather than a claim to settle from Nashville.
    </p>
  </aside>

    <div class="itinerary-story__opening-copy">
      <p>
        Nick described exchanges with manufacturers including WillowWood and
        Fillauer, presentations at AOPA and AAOP meetings, and occasional
        support for research and development. He also fabricates for Amputee
        Blade Runners, a Nashville nonprofit that provides running and activity
        prostheses to athletes at no cost.
      </p>
      <p>
        The overlap was striking. People at FabCo knew the prosthetist I had
        previously shadowed. Companies I recognized from component catalogs
        appeared again as collaborators. In prosthetics, expertise does not sit
        neatly inside one clinic or manufacturer; it travels through calls,
        conferences, difficult cases, and years of accumulated trust.
      </p>
    </div>

  <aside class="itinerary-story__detail">
    <p class="itinerary-story__eyebrow">The reimbursement context</p>
    <h3>A specialized device inside a much larger category</h3>
    <p>
      Under Medicare, artificial limbs are a distinct prosthetic benefit but
      are administered within the broader DMEPOS system—durable medical
      equipment, prosthetics, orthotics, and supplies. Fee schedules and coding
      therefore shape which processes and materials are financially practical.
      I did not find a reliable source for the claim that prosthetics account
      for less than one percent of health-insurance spending, so I have left
      that number out. The more defensible observation is that a small,
      specialized field can receive limited policy attention even when its
      decisions matter enormously to individual users.
    </p>
  </aside>

  <div class="itinerary-story__findings">
    <article>
      <span>01</span>
      <h3>Comfort begins upstream.</h3>
      <p>A trimline, material transition, or small surface defect can later become pressure, restricted motion, or skin irritation.</p>
    </article>
    <article>
      <span>02</span>
      <h3>Consistency is an innovation.</h3>
      <p>A dependable traditional process may serve a wearer better than a newer method that occasionally produces an unpredictable result.</p>
    </article>
    <article>
      <span>03</span>
      <h3>Expertise is shared infrastructure.</h3>
      <p>The finished socket carries knowledge gathered across technicians, clinicians, manufacturers, and previous cases.</p>
    </article>
    <article>
      <span>04</span>
      <h3>The invisible work is personal.</h3>
      <p>The hours spent shaping, trimming, and finishing determine whether someone can move through a full day without thinking constantly about the device.</p>
    </article>
  </div>

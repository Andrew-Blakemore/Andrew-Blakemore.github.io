    <h2>Prosthetic Care in the UK</h2>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">In Iceland, I investigated how certain prosthetic components were designed and manufactured. In the UK, my attention shifted more toward clinical care and the relationship between a prosthetist, physiotherapist, and prosthetic user. Conversations with Dorset Orthopaedic clinicians and a visit to their Amersham clinic gave me a closer look at this relationship.</p>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">These encounters are part of a wider exploration of prosthetic care in the UK, from rehabilitation and funding to veterans’ support and the representation of limb difference in art. Each offers a different perspective on how a prosthesis becomes part of someone’s life.</p>

      <h2>Two Routes to Prosthetic Care</h2>
      <p>
        Prosthetic care in the UK is available through the National Health Service (NHS), the publicly funded healthcare system, and through private providers such as Dorset Orthopaedic. NHS limb centres provide assessment, prostheses, and rehabilitation without charging eligible patients for that care. They support people with many causes of limb loss, including amputations following vascular disease or complications of diabetes, traumatic injuries, and congenital limb differences. Access does not depend on having a compensation claim or the money to pay privately.
      </p>
      <p>
        Private care needs a separate source of funding. At Dorset, the clinicians described a caseload made up largely of people with traumatic injuries, often supported through personal injury compensation claims. Those claims can fund prostheses and rehabilitation through payments from the responsible party or its insurer. Other patients pay out of pocket. This helps explain why the patients and goals encountered in a private clinic may differ from those in an NHS limb centre.
      </p>
      <p>
        Funding also shapes what each service can offer. NHS provision operates within public budgets and eligibility criteria, which can limit the range of components or rehabilitation available. Private funding may allow more options or more intensive support, depending on what is agreed and affordable. That does not mean advanced devices are absent from the NHS: <a href="https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/clin-comm-pol-16061P.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NHS England funds microprocessor knees for patients who meet its criteria</a>.
      </p>
      <p>
        Before visiting Amersham, I met a Dorset physiotherapist over Teams who had previously worked in the NHS. A physiotherapist is what we generally call a physical therapist in the United States. The biggest change in her role from what I had seen in the US, as she described it, was how she worked side by side with a prosthetist in the same room. At Dorset, fitting and rehabilitation could happen together, with both clinicians available to assess the same problem. NHS services can also bring these professions together, although arrangements vary between centres.
      </p>
      <p>
        She described that partnership during rehabilitation and component trials, including trials of microprocessor feet. If someone struggled to walk comfortably, she could assess their movement while the prosthetist checked the socket, alignment, or device settings. They could then see whether an adjustment helped. The patient did not have to carry an explanation from an appointment with their prosthetist to another with their physiotherapist, while each clinician tried to work out what the other had seen.
      </p>
      <p>
        This in-house partnership between a prosthetist and physical therapist was not something I had encountered in the US. As a result, there can be a significant disconnect that hinders effective rehabilitation. I had seen patients spend weeks struggling with discomfort or difficulty putting on a prosthesis because the problem had not reached the person who could address it. I had seen some collaboration between clinics and independent physical therapists, but never together in the same building. When I later took the Tube to Amersham, I wanted to see that partnership in practice.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>What Having a Workshop Changes</h2>
      <p>
        The first thing that surprised me was the size of the building. The clinic where I had interned in Kentucky had only a consultation room and a workshop. The prosthetist made plaster models and assembled and adjusted limbs in the workshop, but sent models to central fabrication facilities in Ohio and Tennessee for the check and definitive sockets. That arrangement kept the workshop smaller, while adding shipping time between stages.
      </p>
      <p>
        At Amersham, both check sockets and definitive sockets were fabricated in-house. Alongside consultation rooms and a physiotherapy gym, the clinic had spaces for plaster work, model rectification, and socket fabrication. Rectification is the process of modifying a model to guide the socket’s fit. Seeing the workspaces helped me understand why incorporating fabrication into a clinic changes both its footprint and the way people work within it.
      </p>
    <figure class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
      <img src="/assets/img/keegan/united-kingdom/amersham-plaster-model-workbench.webp"
        alt="Plaster models mounted along a workbench with sinks and tools in the Amersham workshop." width="2560" height="1920"

      <figcaption>Plaster models and work surfaces in the clinic’s fabrication area. The work of refining fit begins before the finished socket reaches the patient.
        <span class="itinerary-story__source">Photo: author, Dorset Orthopaedic, Amersham.</span>
      </figcaption>

      <p>
        A prosthetist talked me through the fitting process. Like the clinic in Kentucky, the team might work through two or three transparent check sockets while refining a patient’s fit. These temporary sockets allow clinicians to assess contact and comfort before making a costly definitive version. He explained that patients using them outside the clinic needed close review, often weekly, because durability can vary depending on the person’s weight and how they use the limb. In some cases, the team reinforced a check socket with carbon fiber. I had seen fiberglass reinforcement before, but carbon fiber reinforcement was new to me.
      </p>
      <p>
        With a workshop in-house, I was curious if the team could cast someone in the morning and give them their check socket that afternoon. This way, the person could be cast and start walking on a temporary socket all in one day, rather than scheduling a follow-up appointment to receive their socket. The prosthetist said it was possible, although unusual and generally reserved for urgent circumstances. It was not the normal timetable, but having that option can matter a lot.
      </p>
      <p>
        In Kentucky, I had met patients who traveled more than three hours each way for care. Consultation, casting, fitting, and adjustments could mean repeated journeys before a limb was ready for regular use. An appointment’s cost often includes time away from work, transport, and the effort of getting there. Combining stages cannot remove the need to check fit carefully, but it can reduce part of the burden of scheduling multiple appointments. Because of this, I began to think of fabrication capacity as an access issue as well as a manufacturing decision.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>Who Can Reach This Kind of Care?</h2>
      <p>
        Seeing the workshop and rehabilitation spaces made the difference in funding tangible. A clinic may have the equipment and expertise to offer extensive care, but a patient still needs a way to pay for it. For someone with a personal injury claim, a case manager may help coordinate that process. Dorset’s <a href="https://www.dorset-ortho.com/offer/information-for-defendant-insurers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">published referral pathway</a> describes clinical assessment, recommendations, reporting to insurers, and follow-up. This is a different route from relying on an ordinary private medical insurance policy to cover a prosthesis.
      </p>
      <p>
        The team described assessment as a way to understand the person’s life before deciding what services to provide. Work, hobbies, confidence, and the effects of the injury could all shape a plan, including recommendations for physiotherapy, occupational therapy, or psychological support. My understanding was that the clinic and case manager stay in communication as needs change. Receiving a leg is one stage in that process, though it does not necessarily mark the end of rehabilitation.
      </p>
      <p>
        I asked whether maintaining relationships with established patients eventually overwhelmed a clinic that also accepted new referrals. The prosthetist described a changing rhythm of care where appointments might be weekly during early fitting and rehabilitation, then fortnightly as the fit and the patient’s confidence improved. Once someone was comfortable using a definitive prosthesis, reviews could become six-monthly or annual, with additional visits when adjustments were needed. This was the pattern he described, not a fixed timetable for every patient. Keeping someone under the clinic’s care did not mean continuing the intensity of those first few months indefinitely.
      </p>
      <p>
        People might also choose a clinic because of an established relationship rather than simply its distance from home. A team that already understood their history, goals, and prosthetic setup could be worth traveling farther to see. Conversely, someone might seek ongoing adjustments closer to home once their initial rehabilitation was complete. This helped me understand that access was about both geography and continuity because the nearest clinic was not necessarily the one a person felt best understood their needs.
      </p>
      </div>
    </section>
    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
      <h2>The Freedom to Choose a Component</h2>
      <p>
        The physiotherapist told me that although Dorset is part of Ottobock Care, clinicians retain freedom of prescription. In other words, they can recommend components from other manufacturers when those better meet a patient’s needs. That distinction matters because the company that owns a clinic and the clinician choosing a component have different responsibilities and different obligations.
      </p>
      <p>
        Public funding does not always mean that the NHS directly employs the prosthetic team. Some NHS services are delivered by contracted private companies, which may provide prosthetics, orthotics, or both. The patient still receives NHS-funded care, but the company running the service can change if the contract is awarded to a different provider. If that company also manufactures components, it may be both the employer of the clinician choosing a device and the business that benefits from its sale. That overlap makes freedom of prescription especially important.
      </p>
      <p>
        One clinician described how this had played out in a previous NHS-contracted service. Under the original provider, they had been able to prescribe components from different manufacturers. When another company took over the contract, that freedom initially remained. Gradually, however, the new provider began questioning choices outside its own product range. Clinicians then had to complete a form justifying each outside component. According to the clinician, those additional requirements eventually became a directive to prescribe the company’s own products. What began as an administrative change had eventually narrowed the choices they could offer patients.
      </p>
      <p>
        The clinician told me that this loss of prescribing freedom was a reason for leaving that role. I could not independently verify the account, and it should not be taken as a description of every NHS contractor. What stayed with me was how a clinician could remain in the same service while the rules governing their decisions changed around them. If a component from another manufacturer better suited a patient, clinical judgment alone might no longer be enough to make it available.
      </p>
      <p>
        Choice alone does not settle whether a recommendation for a specific device is appropriate. The physiotherapist described using rehabilitation trials and evidence of benefit to support decisions, particularly when considering a more complex or expensive component. A trial involves giving the patient an opportunity to use a proposed component, with fitting and rehabilitation support, before committing to it. The prosthetist can adjust the setup while the physiotherapist helps the person learn how to use it. That gives the team a chance to distinguish an unsuitable device from one the patient simply has not yet learned to use.
      </p>
      <p>
        The question is then whether the component offers a meaningful improvement for that person. Does it help with the movements they find difficult? Can they use its features comfortably and consistently? Does the benefit justify choosing it over a simpler option? These are the kinds of questions a supported trial can help answer. As I understood the physiotherapist’s explanation, the team needed to demonstrate why a recommendation made sense, rather than assume that more features meant better care. Having both clinicians involved also meant the decision could draw on what happened during rehabilitation, as well as what happened at the fitting appointment.
      </p>
      <p>
        A member of the healthcare operations team at the clinic added another perspective. Having began work at Dorset Orthopaedic prior to its acquisition by Ottobock in 2014, she described how joining the Ottobock Care network expanded the support around the clinic. HR, IT, marketing, and shared administrative systems became part of a much larger operation. In her account, the clinical work downstairs did not change much, while the management and administration upstairs changed substantially. The clinic also gained access to a wider network of colleagues and resources.
      </p>
      <p>
        Her earlier experience with Ministry of Defence-related work showed another side of how services are organized. She described contracts in which bidders needed to show more than their ability to manage an ordinary caseload at an agreed price. They also had to address what would happen if conflict intensified and the number of injured service personnel rose sharply. Afghanistan was the example she used: a service needed the capacity to respond to an increase in demand, alongside the ability to deliver care quickly. The contract therefore had to consider how provision could expand when it was needed, rather than assume that patient numbers would remain steady.
      </p>
      <p>
        These conversations made clinic ownership feel more complicated than a choice between independence and scale. A larger network can bring resources that a small practice would struggle to maintain. The question is how those resources support clinicians while preserving their ability to respond to the person in front of them.
      </p>
      </div>
    </section>
    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
      <h2>Fit Before Features</h2>
      <p>
        When I asked the physiotherapist about her opinion on the biggest challenge in the clinical space, she returned to socket fit. This did not surprise me. However capable a knee or foot may be, it is difficult to benefit from it if the connection to the body is uncomfortable. This led us into a conversation about casting and how a socket’s shape is established. She told me that plaster casting was the main approach at Dorset, particularly for below-knee sockets, while noting that other UK services had moved further toward digital scanning.
      </p>
      <p>
        The clinicians anticipated wider use of scanning and digital fabrication, including in partial-hand work, but were cautious about whether the processes available to them could consistently produce the fit they wanted. A scan captures the limb’s surface; clinical judgment is still needed to turn that shape into a socket that distributes pressure appropriately and remains comfortable in use. With plaster, those decisions take shape through the cast and the physical rectification of the model. A digital workflow changes the tools used to make them. I took their caution as a question about reliability within their workflow, rather than proof that scanning is inherently worse than casting. For them, adopting a new method depended on confidence in the resulting fit, not just the speed of capturing a shape.
      </p>
      <p>
        Upper-limb care brought a different set of challenges. The prosthetist estimated that roughly 85% of the clinic’s patients needed lower-limb care and around 15% needed upper-limb care, with much of the latter involving fingers or partial hands. With fewer upper-limb cases, practical experience can become concentrated among just one or two clinicians in a practice. A team may have several prosthetists without every member regularly fitting upper-limb devices.
      </p>
      <p>
        He said Ottobock was looking toward broader expectations that clinicians have a grounding in both upper- and lower-limb care. I understood this as a direction being discussed, rather than an established requirement I could confirm. The aim was not for everyone to become equally expert in every device. It was for colleagues to understand enough to support a patient when the main upper-limb specialist was on holiday, unavailable, or had left the practice. Otherwise, even a well-equipped clinic could find its ability to help tied to the availability of a single person. Specialization brings valuable depth, but the team also needs enough shared knowledge to maintain continuity.
      </p>
      <p>
        I also asked about newer approaches to upper-limb devices, bringing up Open Bionics and Koalaa in a discussion about affordability, weight, and ways of simplifying provision. Part of that conversation concerned soft devices with interchangeable attachments. <a href="https://www.yourkoalaa.com/faq" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Koalaa’s approach</a>, for example, uses a washable fabric sleeve that holds different tools for activities such as writing, cooking, or cycling. The device can help someone perform a particular task without needing to reproduce all the movements of a hand.
      </p>
      <p>
        I had written “off the shelf” in my notes, but that needs some qualification. A product based on an existing sleeve-and-tool design still has to suit the person’s limb and be sized appropriately; Koalaa describes its sleeves as made to the user’s measurements. Its range includes options for below-elbow and partial-hand differences, so one design does not fit every level of limb loss. What interested me was the possibility of making useful assistance easier to obtain through a soft interface and a choice of attachments, rather than through a rigid socket and a hand for every task.
      </p>
      <p>
        Open Bionics represents a different approach within that conversation. Its <a href="https://openbionics.com/hero-arm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Hero Arm</a> is a custom myoelectric prosthesis, using muscle signals to operate a powered hand, with a socket made from a scan of the user’s limb. Its <a href="https://openbionics.com/hero-gauntlet/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Hero Gauntlet</a> is a custom, 3D-printed partial-hand device whose fingers are operated by wrist movement. That distinction matters: 3D printing does not necessarily mean a soft or off-the-shelf prosthesis, and the Gauntlet is itself body-powered.
      </p>
      <p>
        The broader point I took from the discussion was that an attachment designed for a particular task is not a universal substitute for a conventional body-powered arm or hand. It may work alongside another prosthesis, or be the preferred option for a specific activity. Whether it is useful depends on the person’s remaining anatomy, the movements available to them, and what they want to do. Simpler construction can make a device more accessible without making it interchangeable with every other kind of upper-limb prosthesis.
      </p>
      <p>
        We also discussed how a more complex upper-limb prosthesis can combine components from several suppliers. A hand might come from one company, a wrist unit from another, and a control system from a third. They do not all have to share a brand, but they do have to work together: the physical connections, power supply, and control signals must be compatible. Some combinations are supported, while others are restricted by a manufacturer’s design. A clinician therefore cannot select each part in isolation. Choosing a hand may narrow the available wrists or controllers, which makes compatibility another practical limit on freedom of prescription.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>Watching the Conversation Become Care</h2>
      <p>
        Back downstairs, I met a patient who had lost his leg above the knee in a bus accident. He had initially received care through the NHS and later moved to Dorset, where he was working with the physiotherapist.
      </p>
      <p>
        I did not establish why he changed services. One possibility I wondered about was the timing of funding: someone might begin NHS rehabilitation while exploring whether private care could be financed. That was a question prompted by his experience, not an explanation he gave me. What I did know was that his care had crossed between the two settings, making the distinction less tidy than separate descriptions of “NHS patients” and “private patients” might suggest.
      </p>
      <p>
        He told me he had moved from a Blatchford Orion3 knee to an Ottobock C-Leg. I asked whether changing between two microprocessor knees had required much adjustment, and he confirmed that there had been a learning curve. It was a useful reminder that being familiar with one device does not make another immediately intuitive. Learning how a knee responds is part of learning to trust it.
      </p>
      <p>
        He also described difficulties with an earlier pin-lock arrangement and the space it required in his prosthetic setup. I did not have enough information to identify the cause of the problems he experienced. Still, it brought attention to something easy to miss when looking at a component on its own: the suspension system, socket, knee, and foot all have to fit within the space available and work together.
      </p>
    <figure class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
      <img src="/assets/img/keegan/united-kingdom/amersham-gait-training-parallel-bars.webp"

      <p>
        While the physiotherapist led exercises, a prosthetist was making adjustments to the patient’s leg. When she returned with it, he put it on and began walking with both clinicians watching. The physiotherapist moved in front of and behind him, offering cues about his movement. She referred back to the muscles they had just worked on and encouraged him to bring that awareness into his steps. At the same time, the prosthetist asked how the knee’s resistance felt and considered its response during different tasks.
      </p>
      <p>
        Here was the arrangement the physiotherapist had described over Teams, unfolding in front of me. If he found a movement difficult, the people addressing the device and the movement were watching the same attempt. They could hear his feedback, adjust their approach, and see what changed. There was less room for an observation to be lost between appointments.
      </p>
      <p>
        The prosthetist also helped him use the knee’s phone app to explore activity settings. Ottobock describes <a href="https://www.ottobock.com/en-au/mymodes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">MyModes</a> as additional configurations developed with a clinician and selected through the Cockpit app or prescribed movement patterns. The prosthetist sets up the modes; the user can then switch between those available to them. This lets a knee behave differently for a particular activity without asking the user to program the device from scratch.
      </p>
      <p>
        During the appointment, they found a setting I noted as “table tennis” and used it for a step exercise with the physiotherapist. What caught my attention was how a setting associated with one activity could help with another. As I understood the exercise, the added support kept the knee from giving way when his foot placement was imperfect, allowing him time to regain control and correct the movement. I did not record the configuration precisely enough to give a locking angle or describe it as a standard preset. But the interaction itself was clear: the prosthetist explained and adjusted the device’s behavior, and the physiotherapist turned that behavior into an opportunity to practice.
      </p>
      <p>
        The technology and rehabilitation were being adjusted together. The prosthetist helped make the knee’s behavior understandable, and the physiotherapist connected that behavior to an exercise. Another comment from the visit stayed with me: discomfort was something to bring to the team, rather than simply push through. That makes particular sense in a setting where several people can help work out what the discomfort is telling them.
      </p>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
      <h2>Support Beyond the Clinic</h2>
      <p>
        The physiotherapist also mentioned organizations that support people beyond their appointments. These are starting points for further learning, rather than organizations I have all visited.
      </p>
      <ul>
        <li><a href="https://www.blesma.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Blesma</a>: support for limbless veterans; a separate conversation is planned.</li>
        <li><a href="https://limbless-association.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Limbless Association</a>: information, advice, and peer support for people living with limb loss.</li>
        <li><a href="https://www.limbpower.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">LimbPower</a>: opportunities and resources for physical activity, sport, and the arts.</li>
        <li><a href="https://amputationfoundation.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Amputation Foundation</a>: support and resources for people affected by amputation.</li>
        <li>Steel Bones: another support organization the physiotherapist recommended I explore.</li>
      </ul>
      </div>
    </section>
    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
      <h2>What I Took Away</h2>
      <p>
        In Iceland, I had been drawn to the coordination of sensors, software, and mechanical parts. Amersham showed me another kind of coordination: a socket being adjusted while rehabilitation continued, a clinician explaining a setting, and a patient testing whether it helped. The device remained central, but its usefulness depended on these exchanges.
      </p>
      <p>
        The physiotherapist also pointed toward changes she hoped to see, including developments in osseointegration, digital methods, and devices that are less bulky or demanding to charge. Those conversations will be worth returning to. For now, the most convincing example of progress was already happening in the room: the right people could respond to the same problem together.
      </p>

      <h2>Who Decides How a Body Is Seen?</h2>
      <p>
        At the V&amp;A, I came across several self-portraits by Japanese multimedia artist Mari Katayama, from her series titled <em>tree of life</em>. As a photographer myself, I was fixated on the way the mirrors repeated and fragmented her body, making it difficult to tell where one reflection ended and another began.
      </p>
      <p>
        The series grew from the artist’s interest in how images can be reproduced and reshaped, and how other people’s expectations shape our relationship with our bodies. She built the mirrored setting in her studio and photographed herself, taking control of both sides of the camera. As <a href="https://www.yutakakikutakegallery.com/exhibitions/tree-of-life/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">her gallery’s account of the work</a> explains, that choice also explores the power a photographer holds over the person being photographed.
      </p>

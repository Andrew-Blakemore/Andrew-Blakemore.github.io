    <h2>The Care Around the Prosthesis</h2>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">In Iceland, I had looked inside prosthetic devices. In the UK, my attention shifted toward the care around them: who fits a prosthesis, who helps someone learn to use it, and how those people work together. Conversations with Dorset Orthopaedic clinicians and a visit to their Amersham clinic gave me a closer look at that relationship.</p>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">These encounters are part of a wider exploration of prosthetic care in the UK, from rehabilitation and funding to veterans’ support and the representation of limb difference in art. Each offers a different perspective on how a prosthesis becomes part of someone’s life.</p>

      <h2>A Conversation, Then a Clinic</h2>
      <p>
        Before visiting Amersham, I met Kat, a physiotherapist at Dorset Orthopaedic, over Teams. She had previously worked in an NHS service before moving into private care. I wanted to understand what that move had changed about her work. Much of our conversation came back to something deceptively simple: having a prosthetist nearby.
      </p>
      <p>
        Kat described working alongside prosthetists during component trials and rehabilitation, including trials of microprocessor feet. If a patient struggled with a movement, they could assess it together. The physiotherapist could explain what she was seeing, and the prosthetist could consider whether the fit, alignment, or component settings contributed to it. A question did not have to wait for a message to reach another practice.
      </p>
      <p>
        That stood out because of situations I had encountered in the US. Sometimes a patient’s discomfort began with something as basic as difficulty putting the prosthesis on correctly. If that problem went unresolved, wearing the device became less appealing, and rehabilitation suffered. A therapist unfamiliar with that particular prosthesis might struggle to identify what needed changing. The patient could spend weeks working around a problem that someone else on their care team knew how to address.
      </p>
      <p>
        I had seen collaboration in American clinics, including relationships with independent physical therapists and practices located alongside vascular specialists. What felt different in Kat’s account was the immediacy of it. Later, when I took the Tube to Amersham, I wanted to see how that worked in an ordinary appointment.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>What Having a Workshop Changes</h2>
      <p>
        The first thing that surprised me was the size of the building. The clinic where I had interned in Lexington, Kentucky, had two main spaces: a consultation room and a workshop. The prosthetist made plaster models and assembled and adjusted limbs there, but sent models to central fabrication facilities for the check and definitive sockets. That arrangement kept the local workshop smaller, while adding shipping time between stages.
      </p>
      <p>
        At Amersham, check sockets and definitive sockets were fabricated in-house. Alongside consultation rooms and a physiotherapy gym, the clinic had spaces for plaster work, model modification, and socket fabrication. Rectification is the process of modifying a model to guide the socket’s fit. Seeing the workspaces helped me understand why bringing fabrication into a clinic changes both its footprint and the way people work within it.
      </p>
    <figure class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
      <img src="/assets/img/keegan/united-kingdom/amersham-plaster-model-workbench.webp"
        alt="Plaster models mounted along a workbench with sinks and tools in the Amersham workshop." width="2560" height="1920"
        loading="lazy" decoding="async"
        style="display: block; width: 100%; height: auto; max-height: none; aspect-ratio: auto; object-fit: contain;">
      <figcaption>Plaster models and work surfaces in the clinic’s fabrication area. The work of refining fit begins before the finished socket reaches the patient.
        <span class="itinerary-story__source">Photo: Andrew Blakemore, Dorset Orthopaedic, Amersham.</span>
      </figcaption>
    </figure>
      <p>
        A prosthetist, Abdul, talked me through the fitting process. Like the clinic in Kentucky, the team might work through two or three transparent check sockets while refining a patient’s fit. These temporary sockets allow clinicians to assess contact and comfort before making a definitive version. He explained that patients using them outside the clinic needed close review, often weekly, because durability depends on the material, the person’s weight, and how they use the limb. In some cases, the team reinforced a check socket with carbon fiber. I had seen fiberglass reinforcement before, but this was new to me.
      </p>
      <p>
        Having the workshop nearby prompted a practical question: could someone be cast in the morning and receive a check socket that afternoon? Abdul said it was possible, although unusual and generally reserved for urgent circumstances. It was not the normal timetable, but having that option mattered to me.
      </p>
      <p>
        In Kentucky, I had met patients traveling more than three hours each way for care. Consultation, casting, fitting, and adjustments could mean repeated journeys before a limb was ready for regular use. An appointment’s cost includes time away from work, transport, and the effort of getting there. Combining stages cannot remove the need to check fit carefully, but it can reduce some of that burden. I began to think of fabrication capacity as an access issue as well as a manufacturing decision.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>Who Can Reach This Kind of Care?</h2>
      <p>
        The building also made the funding question harder to ignore. Dorset Orthopaedic provides private care, while NHS limb services offer a publicly funded route. The clinicians described a private caseload strongly shaped by traumatic injuries and personal injury claims. That helps explain why their patients and rehabilitation goals might differ from those encountered in a service caring for people across a wider range of causes of limb loss. It would be misleading to compare the two settings without considering who reaches each one.
      </p>
      <p>
        In a personal injury case, funding for private rehabilitation may come through a compensation claim or arrangements with an insurer. That is different from assuming ordinary private medical insurance will cover a prosthesis. Dorset’s <a href="https://www.dorset-ortho.com/offer/information-for-defendant-insurers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">published referral pathway</a> describes referrals made directly or through a case manager, followed by clinical assessment, recommendations, reporting, and review. Private care can also be self-funded, making a person’s financial circumstances another part of the picture.
      </p>
      <p>
        The team described assessment as a way to understand the person’s life before deciding what to provide. Work, hobbies, confidence, and the effects of the injury could all shape a plan, including recommendations for physiotherapy, occupational therapy, or psychological support. My understanding was that the clinic and case manager stay in communication as needs change. Receiving a leg is one stage in that process; it does not necessarily mark the end of rehabilitation.
      </p>
      <p>
        The contrast also needs care on the NHS side. Microprocessor knees are not exclusive to private practice: <a href="https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/clin-comm-pol-16061P.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NHS England’s commissioning policy</a> provides for them under specified criteria and describes multidisciplinary assessment and rehabilitation. The question is which options a particular person can access, through which service, and with what support. Arrangements also vary across the UK, so I cannot treat what I heard in Amersham as a single national model.
      </p>
      <p>
        I asked whether maintaining relationships with established patients eventually overwhelmed a clinic that also accepted new referrals. Abdul described a changing rhythm of care: frequent appointments during early fitting and rehabilitation, followed by more widely spaced reviews once someone was comfortable using their prosthesis. People might also choose a clinic because of an established relationship rather than simply its distance from home. Continuity had value, but it did not require the same intensity of care at every stage.
      </p>
      </div>
    </section>
    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
      <h2>The Freedom to Choose a Component</h2>
      <p>
        Kat told me that although Dorset is part of Ottobock Care, clinicians retain freedom of prescription: they can recommend components from other manufacturers when those better meet a patient’s needs. That distinction matters because the company that owns a clinic and the clinician choosing a component have different responsibilities.
      </p>
      <p>
        One clinician described a previous NHS-contracted service where, after a change of provider, prescribing outside the provider’s own product range became progressively harder. In their account, questions about individual choices developed into additional justification requirements and eventually restrictions on what they could prescribe. I could not independently verify that history, but the clinician described it as a factor in leaving that role. It raised a question I want to carry into other visits: how much choice does a clinician have in practice, beyond what a service says it offers?
      </p>
      <p>
        Choice alone does not settle whether a recommendation is appropriate. Kat described using trials, rehabilitation, and evidence of benefit to support decisions. We discussed the risk of recommending a more complex or expensive component than someone needs. A trial can help test whether additional features make a useful difference for that person, rather than treating sophistication as a benefit in itself.
      </p>
      <p>
        Ankita, who works in healthcare operations, added another perspective. She described how joining a larger organization had expanded the support around the clinic, including HR, IT, marketing, and shared administrative systems. The clinical work could feel familiar while the organization supporting it changed substantially. Her earlier experience with Ministry of Defence-related work also brought questions of capacity and responsiveness into the discussion: a service needs to function when demand changes, not only when it is predictable.
      </p>
      <p>
        These conversations made ownership feel more complicated than a choice between independence and scale. A larger network can bring resources that a small practice would struggle to maintain. The question is how those resources support clinicians while preserving their ability to respond to the person in front of them.
      </p>
      </div>
    </section>
    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
      <h2>Fit Before Features</h2>
      <p>
        When I asked Kat about the biggest clinical challenge, she returned to socket fit. That was familiar from my time in the US. However capable a knee or foot may be, it is difficult to benefit from it if the connection to the body is uncomfortable. She described plaster casting as central to their current work, alongside interest in more digital methods.
      </p>
      <p>
        The clinicians anticipated wider use of scanning and digital fabrication, but were cautious about whether a particular process could consistently produce the fit they wanted. I took that as a question about reliability within their workflow, rather than proof that scanning is inherently worse than casting. A digital shape still has to become a socket someone can tolerate under load. The useful comparison is what happens through the whole fitting process, including how easily problems can be corrected.
      </p>
      <p>
        Upper-limb care brought a different set of challenges. Abdul described it as a smaller part of the clinic’s work, with many of those cases involving fingers or partial hands. That can concentrate experience among a few clinicians. We discussed the importance of colleagues having enough familiarity to support a patient when the main specialist is unavailable, even if they do not all have the same depth of expertise.
      </p>
      <p>
        I also asked about newer approaches to upper-limb devices. These should not be grouped together as one-size-fits-all alternatives: Open Bionics, for example, describes its <a href="https://openbionics.com/hero-arm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Hero Arm</a> as a custom myoelectric prosthesis manufactured using a scan of the user’s limb. The conversation reminded me to distinguish a fabrication method from the type of device it produces. Likewise, combining a hand, wrist, and control system from different suppliers depends on their compatibility, not simply on each component’s individual capabilities.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>Watching the Conversation Become Care</h2>
      <p>
        Back downstairs, I met Josh, who had lost his leg above the knee in a traffic accident about a year earlier. Before the accident, he had worked as a television camera operator. He had initially received care through the NHS and later moved to Dorset, where he was working with Kat.
      </p>
      <p>
        I did not establish why he changed services. One possibility I wondered about was the timing of funding: someone might begin NHS rehabilitation while exploring whether private care could be financed. That was a question prompted by his experience, not an explanation he gave me. What I did know was that his care had crossed between the two settings, making the distinction less tidy than separate descriptions of “NHS patients” and “private patients” might suggest.
      </p>
      <p>
        Josh told me he had moved from a Blatchford Orion3 knee to an Ottobock C-Leg. I asked whether changing between two microprocessor knees had required much adjustment, and he confirmed that there had been a learning curve. It was a useful reminder that being familiar with one device does not make another immediately intuitive. Learning how a knee responds is part of learning to trust it.
      </p>
      <p>
        He also described difficulties with an earlier pin-lock arrangement and the space it required in his prosthetic setup. I did not have enough information to identify the cause of the problems he experienced. Still, it brought attention to something easy to miss when looking at a component on its own: the suspension system, socket, knee, and foot all have to fit within the space available and work together.
      </p>
    <figure class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
      <img src="/assets/img/keegan/united-kingdom/amersham-gait-training-parallel-bars.webp"
        alt="Parallel bars, a wheelchair and a wall mirror in the clinic’s gait-training area." width="2560" height="1920"

      </figcaption>
    </figure>
      <p>
        While Kat led exercises, a prosthetist was making adjustments to Josh’s leg. When she returned with it, he put it on and began walking with both clinicians watching. Kat moved in front of and behind him, offering cues about his movement. She referred back to the muscles they had just worked on and encouraged him to bring that awareness into his steps. At the same time, the prosthetist asked how the knee’s resistance felt and considered its response during different tasks.
      </p>
      <p>
        Here was the arrangement Kat had described over Teams, unfolding in front of me. If Josh found a movement difficult, the people addressing the device and the movement were watching the same attempt. They could hear his feedback, adjust their approach, and see what changed. There was less room for an observation to be lost between appointments.
      </p>
      <p>
        The prosthetist also helped Josh use the knee’s phone app to explore activity settings. Ottobock describes <a href="https://www.ottobock.com/en-au/mymodes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">MyModes</a> as additional settings configured with a clinician, which users can switch between through the Cockpit app or prescribed movement patterns. During this session, the team adapted an activity setting for a step exercise. I did not record the exact configuration reliably enough to explain its mechanics, but I could see the purpose: giving Josh support while he practiced the movement Kat was teaching.
      </p>
      <p>
        The technology and rehabilitation were being adjusted together. The prosthetist helped make the knee’s behavior understandable, and Kat connected that behavior to an exercise. Another comment from the visit stayed with me: discomfort was something to bring to the team, rather than simply push through. That makes particular sense in a setting where several people can help work out what the discomfort is telling them.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>What I Took Away</h2>
      <p>
        In Iceland, I had been drawn to the coordination of sensors, software, and mechanical parts. Amersham showed me another kind of coordination: a socket being adjusted while rehabilitation continued, a clinician explaining a setting, and a patient testing whether it helped. The device remained central, but its usefulness depended on these exchanges.
      </p>
      <p>
        Kat also pointed toward changes she hoped to see, including developments in osseointegration, digital methods, and devices that are less bulky or demanding to charge. Those conversations will be worth returning to. For now, the most convincing example of progress was already happening in the room: the right people could respond to the same problem together.
      </p>

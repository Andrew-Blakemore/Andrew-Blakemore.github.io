    <h2>Prosthetic Care in the UK</h2>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">In Iceland, I investigated how certain prosthetic components were designed and manufactured. In the UK, my attention shifted more toward clinical care and the dynamic between a prosthetist, physiotherapist, and prosthetic user. Conversations with Dorset Orthopaedic clinicians and a visit to their Amersham clinic gave me a closer look at this relationship.</p>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">These encounters are part of a wider exploration of prosthetic care in the UK, from rehabilitation and funding to veterans’ support and the representation of limb difference in art. Each offers a different perspective on how a prosthesis becomes part of someone’s life.</p>

      <h2>Two Routes to Prosthetic Care</h2>
      <p>
        Prosthetic care in the UK is available through the National Health Service (NHS), the publicly funded healthcare system, and through private providers such as Dorset Orthopaedic. NHS limb centres provide assessment, prostheses, and rehabilitation without charging eligible patients for that care. They support people with many causes of limb loss, including amputations following vascular disease or complications of diabetes, traumatic injuries, and congenital limb differences. Access does not depend on having a compensation claim or the money to pay privately.
      </p>
      <p>
        Private care needs a separate source of funding. At Dorset, the clinicians described a caseload made up largely of people with traumatic injuries, often supported through personal injury compensation claims. Those claims can fund prostheses and rehabilitation through payments from the responsible party or its insurer. Other patients pay out of pocket. This helps explain why the patients and goals encountered in a private clinic may differ from those in an NHS limb centre.
      </p>
      <p>
        Funding also shapes what each service can offer. NHS provision operates within public budgets and eligibility criteria, which can limit the range of components or rehabilitation available. Private funding may allow more options or more intensive support, depending on what is agreed and affordable. That does not mean advanced devices are absent from the NHS: <a href="https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/clin-comm-pol-16061P.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NHS England funds microprocessor knees for patients who meet its criteria</a>.
      </p>
      <p>
        Before visiting Amersham, I met over Teams with a Dorset physiotherapist who had previously worked in the NHS. As she described her work, I noted that the biggest difference between her role and what I had seen in the US was that she and the prosthetist treated patients side by side in the same room. At Dorset, fitting and rehabilitation typically happened together, with both clinicians available to assess the same problem. While some NHS services use a similar setup, others separate the prosthetist and physiotherapist into different clinical spaces, even if they share the same building. Arrangements vary significantly between NHS centres.
      </p>
      <p>
        She described how that partnership supported rehabilitation and component trials. If someone struggled to walk comfortably, she could assess their movement while the prosthetist checked the socket or alignment. They could then try various adjustments and determine which helped the most. The patient did not have to carry an explanation from an appointment with their prosthetist to another with their physiotherapist while each clinician tried to work out what the other had seen.
      </p>
      <p>
        I had not previously encountered an in-house partnership between a prosthetist and physical therapist in the US. Without this integrated model, care was often siloed, creating a communication disconnect that hindered effective rehabilitation. I had seen patients spend weeks struggling with discomfort or difficulty putting on a prosthesis because the problem had not reached the person who could address it. While I had seen some collaboration between clinics and independent physical therapists, the clinicians never worked together in the same building. When I later took the Tube to Amersham, I wanted to see that partnership in practice.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>A Clinic That Does It All</h2>
      <p>
        The first thing that surprised me was the size of the building. The clinic where I had interned in Kentucky had only a consultation room and a workshop. The prosthetist made plaster models and assembled and adjusted limbs in the workshop, but sent models to central fabrication facilities in Ohio and Tennessee for the check and definitive sockets. That arrangement kept the workshop smaller while also adding shipping time between stages.
      </p>
      <p>
        At Amersham, both check sockets and definitive sockets were fabricated in-house. Alongside consultation rooms and a physiotherapy gym, the clinic had spaces for plaster work, model rectification, and socket fabrication. Rectification is the process of modifying a model to guide the socket’s fit. Seeing the workspaces helped me understand why incorporating fabrication into a clinic changes both its footprint and the way people work within it.
      </p>

    <br>

      <p>
        A prosthetist talked me through the fitting process. As at the clinic in Kentucky, the team might work through two or three transparent check sockets while refining a patient’s fit. These temporary sockets allow clinicians to assess contact and comfort before making a costly definitive version. He explained that patients using them outside the clinic needed close review, often weekly, because the durability of the material can vary depending on the person’s weight and how they use the limb. In some cases, the team reinforced a check socket with carbon fiber for extra support. I had seen fiberglass reinforcement before, but carbon fiber reinforcement was new to me.
      </p>
      <p>
        With a workshop in-house, I was curious if the team could cast someone in the morning and give them their check socket that afternoon. This way, the person could be cast and start walking on a temporary socket all in one day, rather than needing to schedule a follow-up appointment to receive their socket. The prosthetist said it was possible, although unusual and generally reserved for urgent circumstances. It was not the normal timetable, but having that option can matter a lot.
      </p>
      <p>
        In Kentucky, I had met patients who traveled more than three hours each way for care. Consultation, casting, fitting, and adjustments could mean several journeys to the clinic and back before a limb was ready for regular use. An appointment’s cost often includes time away from work and transport. Combining stages, such as casting and check socket fabrication, cannot remove the need to check fit carefully, but it can reduce part of the burden of scheduling multiple appointments. Because of this, I began to think of fabrication capacity as an access issue as well as a manufacturing decision.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>Who Can Reach This Kind of Care?</h2>
      <p>
        Seeing the workshop and rehabilitation spaces made the difference in funding tangible. A clinic may have the equipment and expertise to offer extensive care, but a patient still needs a way to pay for it. For someone with a personal injury claim, a case manager may help coordinate that process. Dorset’s <a href="https://www.dorset-ortho.com/offer/information-for-defendant-insurers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">published referral pathway</a> describes clinical assessment, recommendations, reporting to insurers, and follow-up. This is a different route from relying on an ordinary private medical insurance policy to cover a prosthesis.
      </p>
      <p>
        The team described an assessment as a way to understand the person’s life before deciding what services to provide. Work, hobbies, confidence, and the effects of the injury could all shape a plan, including recommendations for physiotherapy, occupational therapy, or psychological support. My understanding was that the clinic and case manager stay in communication as needs change. Receiving a leg is one stage in that process, though it does not necessarily mark the end of rehabilitation.
      </p>
      <p>
        I asked whether maintaining relationships with established patients eventually overwhelmed a clinic that also accepted new referrals. The prosthetist described a changing rhythm of care in which appointments might be weekly during early fitting and rehabilitation, then fortnightly as the fit and the patient’s confidence improved. Once someone was comfortable using a definitive prosthesis, reviews could become six-monthly or annual, with additional visits when adjustments were needed. The pattern he described was not a fixed timetable for every patient, but it does highlight how keeping someone under the clinic’s care does not mean continuing the intensity of those first few months indefinitely.
      </p>
      <p>
        People might also choose a clinic because of an established relationship rather than simply its distance from home. A team that already understands their history, goals, and prosthetic device could be worth traveling farther to see. Conversely, someone might seek ongoing adjustments closer to home once their initial rehabilitation is complete. This helped me understand that access was about both geography and continuity because the nearest clinic is not necessarily the one a person feels best understands their needs.
      </p>
      </div>
    </section>
    <section class="itinerary-story__section">
      <div>
      <h2>The Freedom to Prescribe</h2>
      <p>
        The physiotherapist told me that although Dorset is part of Ottobock Care, clinicians retain freedom of prescription. In other words, they can recommend components from other manufacturers when those better meet a patient’s needs. That distinction matters because the company that owns a clinic and the clinician choosing a component have different responsibilities and different priorities.
      </p>
      <p>
        Public funding does not always mean that the NHS directly employs the prosthetic team. Some NHS services are delivered by contracted private companies, which may provide prosthetics, orthotics, or both. The patient still receives NHS-funded care, but the company running the service can change if the contract is awarded to a different provider. If that company also manufactures components, it may be both the employer of the clinician choosing a device and the business that benefits from its sale. That overlap makes freedom of prescription especially important.
      </p>
      <p>
        One clinician described how this had played out in a previous NHS-contracted service. Under the original provider, they had been able to prescribe components from different manufacturers. When another company took over the contract, that freedom initially remained. Gradually, however, the new provider began questioning choices outside its own product range. Clinicians then had to complete a form justifying each outside component. According to the clinician, those additional requirements eventually became a directive to prescribe the company’s own products. What began as an administrative change had eventually narrowed the choices they could offer patients.
      </p>
      <p>
        The clinician told me that this loss of prescribing freedom was a reason for leaving that role. I was particularly fascinated by how a clinician could remain in the same service while the rules governing their decisions changed around them. If a component from another manufacturer better suited a patient, clinical judgment alone might no longer be enough to make it available.
      </p>
      <p>
        Choice alone does not settle whether a recommendation for a specific device is appropriate. The physiotherapist described using rehabilitation trials and evidence of benefit to support decisions, particularly when considering a more complex or expensive component. A trial involves giving the patient an opportunity to use a proposed component, with fitting and rehabilitation support, before committing to it. The prosthetist can adjust the setup while the physiotherapist helps the person learn how to use it. That gives the team a chance to distinguish an unsuitable device from one the patient simply has not yet learned to use.
      </p>
      <p>
        The question is then whether the component offers a meaningful improvement for that person. Does it help with the movements they find difficult? Can they use its features comfortably and consistently? Does the benefit justify choosing it over a simpler option? These are the kinds of questions a supported trial can help answer. As I understood the physiotherapist’s explanation, the team needed to demonstrate why a recommendation made sense, rather than assume that more features meant better care. Having both a prosthetist and physiotherapist involved also meant the decision could draw on what happened during rehabilitation as well as what happened at the fitting appointment.
      </p>
      <p>
        A member of the healthcare operations team at the clinic added another perspective. Having begun work at Dorset Orthopaedic prior to its acquisition by Ottobock in 2014, she described how joining the Ottobock Care network expanded the support around the clinic. HR, IT, marketing, and shared administrative systems became part of a much larger operation. In her account, the excellent clinical work downstairs did not change much, while the management and administration upstairs changed substantially. The clinic also gained access to a wider network of colleagues and resources.
      </p>
      <p>
        Her earlier experience with Ministry of Defence-related work showed another side of how services are organized. She described contracts in which bidders needed to show more than their ability to manage an ordinary caseload at an agreed price. They also had to address factors like what would happen if conflict intensified and the number of injured service personnel rose sharply. She referenced the conflict in Afghanistan as an example of how a service needed the capacity to respond to an increase in demand, alongside the ability to deliver care quickly. The contract therefore had to consider how provision could expand when it was needed, rather than assume that patient numbers would remain steady.
      </p>
      <p>
        These conversations made clinic ownership feel more complicated than a choice between independence and scale. A larger network can bring resources that a small practice would struggle to maintain. The question is how those resources support clinicians while preserving their ability to respond to the person in front of them.
      </p>
      </div>
    </section>

      <div>
      <h2>Fit Before Features</h2>
      <p>
        When I asked the physiotherapist about her opinion on the biggest challenge in the clinical space, she returned to socket fit. This did not surprise me. However capable a knee or foot may be, it is difficult to benefit from it if the connection to the body is uncomfortable. This led us into a conversation about casting and how a socket’s shape is established. She told me that plaster casting was the main approach at Dorset, particularly for below-knee sockets, while noting that some other UK services had moved further toward digital scanning in recent years.
      </p>
      <p>
        The clinicians anticipated wider use of scanning and digital fabrication in the prosthetics industry, but were cautious about whether the processes available to them could consistently produce the fit they wanted. A scan captures the limb’s surface, but clinical expertise is still needed to turn that shape into a socket that distributes pressure appropriately and remains comfortable in use. With plaster, those decisions take shape through the cast and the physical rectification of the model. A digital workflow changes the tools used to make them. I took their caution as a question about reliability within their workflow, rather than proof that scanning is inherently worse than casting. For them, adopting a new method depended on confidence in the resulting fit and whether they could rely on the technology for consistent results.
      </p>
      <p>
        Upper-limb care brings a different set of challenges. The prosthetist estimated that roughly 85% of the clinic’s patients needed lower-limb care and around 15% needed upper-limb care, with much of the latter involving fingers or partial hands. With fewer upper-limb cases, practical experience can become concentrated among just one or two clinicians in a practice. A team may have several prosthetists without every member regularly fitting upper-limb devices.
      </p>
      <p>
        I also asked about newer approaches to upper-limb devices, bringing up Open Bionics and Koalaa in a discussion about affordability, weight, and ways of simplifying provision. Part of that conversation concerned soft devices with interchangeable attachments. <a href="https://www.yourkoalaa.com/faq" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Koalaa’s approach</a>, for example, uses a washable fabric sleeve that holds different tools for activities such as writing, cooking, or cycling. The device can help someone perform a particular task without needing to reproduce all the movements of a hand. Open Bionics represents a different approach. Its <a href="https://openbionics.com/hero-arm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Hero Arm</a> is a custom myoelectric prosthesis, using muscle signals to operate a powered hand, with a socket made from a scan of the user’s limb. Its <a href="https://openbionics.com/hero-gauntlet/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Hero Gauntlet</a> is a custom, 3D-printed partial-hand device whose fingers are operated by wrist movement.
      </p>
      <p>
        The broader point I took from this discussion was that an attachment designed for a particular task is not a universal substitute for a conventional body-powered arm or hand. It may work alongside another prosthesis, or be the preferred option for a specific activity. Whether it is useful depends on the person’s remaining anatomy and what they want to do. Simpler construction can make a device more accessible without making it interchangeable with every other kind of upper-limb prosthesis.
      </p>
      <p>
        We also discussed how a more complex upper-limb prosthesis can combine components from several suppliers. A hand might come from one company, a wrist unit from another, and a control system from a third. They do not all have to share a brand, but they do have to work together; the physical connections, power supply, and control signals must be compatible. Some combinations are supported, while others are restricted by a manufacturer’s design. A clinician therefore cannot select each part in isolation. Choosing a hand may narrow the available wrists or controllers, which makes compatibility another practical limit on freedom of prescription.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>Watching the Conversation Become Care</h2>
      <p>
        Back downstairs, I met a patient who had lost his leg above the knee in a bus accident. He had initially received care through the NHS and later moved to Dorset, where he was working with the physiotherapist. I did not establish why he changed services. I suspect it may have been for financial reasons—someone might begin NHS rehabilitation while waiting for a personal injury settlement or court-ordered funding to clear—but I do not know for sure.
      </p>
      <p>
        He told me he had moved from a Blatchford Orion3 knee to an Ottobock C-Leg. I asked whether changing between two microprocessor knees had required much adjustment, and he confirmed that there had been a learning curve. This made a lot of sense. Every microprocessor knee operates differently, so mastery of one device does not make another immediately intuitive.
      </p>
      <p>
        He also mentioned how he had originally been fitted with a pin-lock suspension system before switching over to a suction suspension system. The original pin-lock system created complications because the extra pin length lowered his knee's center of rotation, hindering his ability to walk and sit. The suction suspension resolved these biomechanical issues and greatly improved his comfort throughout the day.
      </p>

    <br>

      <p>
        While the physiotherapist led exercises, a prosthetist made adjustments to the patient’s leg upstairs. When she returned with it, he put it on and began walking with both clinicians watching. The physiotherapist moved in front of and behind him, offering cues about his movement. She referred back to the muscles they had just worked on and encouraged him to bring that awareness into his steps. At the same time, the prosthetist asked how the knee’s resistance felt and considered its response during different tasks.
      </p>
      <p>
        Here was the arrangement the physiotherapist had described over Teams, unfolding in front of me. If he found a movement difficult, the people addressing the device and the movement were watching the same motion. They could respond to his feedback, adjust their approach, and see what changed. There was much less of an opportunity for an observation to be lost between appointments.
      </p>
      <p>
        The prosthetist also helped him use the knee’s phone app to explore activity settings. Ottobock describes <a href="https://www.ottobock.com/en-au/mymodes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">MyModes</a> as additional configurations developed with a clinician and selected through the Cockpit app or prescribed movement patterns. The prosthetist sets up the modes, and the user can then switch between those available to them. This allows a knee to behave differently for a particular activity without asking the user to program the device from scratch.
      </p>
      <p>
        During the appointment, they found a preset setting labeled as “table tennis” and used it for a step exercise with the physiotherapist. As I understood the exercise, this particular preset mode locked the knee at a certain angle and kept it from giving way when his foot placement was imperfect, allowing him time to regain control and correct the movement. It was interesting to watch the prosthetist explain and adjust the device’s behavior while the physiotherapist turned that behavior into an opportunity to practice exercises.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>What I Took Away</h2>
      <p>
        In Iceland, I had been drawn to the coordination of mechanical parts and software. Amersham showed me another kind of coordination, in this case between a prosthetist and physiotherapist. The device remained central, but its effectiveness depended on this relationship. Before I left, the physiotherapist pointed toward changes she hoped to see shape the prosthetic landscape in the near future, including developments in osseointegration, scanning technology, device robustness, and battery life. Those conversations will be worth returning to.
      </p>

      <h2>How We Choose to Be Seen</h2>
      <p>
        At the V&amp;A, I came across several self-portraits by Japanese multimedia artist Mari Katayama, from her series titled <em>tree of life</em>.
      </p>
      <p>
        The series grew from the artist’s interest in how images can be reproduced and reshaped, and how other people’s expectations shape our relationship with our bodies. She built the mirrored setting in her studio and photographed herself, taking control of both sides of the camera. As <a href="https://www.yutakakikutakegallery.com/exhibitions/tree-of-life/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">her gallery’s account of the work</a> explains, that choice also explores the power a photographer holds over the person being photographed.
      </p>
      </div>

      <br>

These images prompted a different question: how much room do we leave for that person to decide how they are seen? The artist’s photographs invite attention without offering a simple story about limb loss. For me, their impact lies in that refusal to make a body immediately explainable.

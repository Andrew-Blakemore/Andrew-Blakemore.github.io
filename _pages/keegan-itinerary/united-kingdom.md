    <h2>Prosthetic Care in the UK</h2>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">In Iceland, I investigated how certain prosthetic components were designed and manufactured. In the UK, my attention shifted more toward clinical care and the relationship between a prosthetist, physiotherapist, and prosthetic user. Conversations with Dorset Orthopaedic clinicians and a visit to their Amersham clinic gave me a closer look at this relationship.</p>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">These encounters are part of a wider exploration of prosthetic care in the UK, from rehabilitation and funding to veterans’ support and the representation of limb difference in art. Each offers a different perspective on how a prosthesis becomes part of someone’s life.</p>

      <h2>Two Routes to Prosthetic Care</h2>
      <p>
        Prosthetic care in the UK is available through the National Health Service (NHS), the publicly funded healthcare system, and through private providers such as Dorset Orthopaedic. NHS limb centres provide assessment, prostheses, and rehabilitation without charging eligible patients for that care. They support people with many causes of limb loss, including amputations following vascular disease or complications of diabetes, traumatic injuries, and congenital limb differences. Access does not depend on having a compensation claim or the money to pay privately.
      </p>
      <p>
        Private care needs a separate source of funding. At Dorset, the clinicians described a caseload made up largely of people with traumatic injuries, often supported through personal injury compensation claims. Those claims can fund prostheses and rehabilitation through payments from the responsible party or its insurer. Other patients pay out of pocket. This helps explain why the patients and goals encountered in a private clinic may differ from those in an NHS limb centre.
      </p>
      <p>
        Funding also shapes what each service can offer. NHS provision operates within public budgets and eligibility criteria, which can limit the range of components or rehabilitation available. Private funding may allow more options or more intensive support, depending on what is agreed and affordable. That does not mean advanced devices are absent from the NHS: <a href="https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/clin-comm-pol-16061P.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NHS England funds microprocessor knees for patients who meet its criteria</a>.
      </p>
      <p>
        Before visiting Amersham, I met a Dorset physiotherapist over Teams who had previously worked in the NHS. A physiotherapist is what we generally call a physical therapist in the United States. The biggest change she described was working side by side with a prosthetist, the clinician who fits and adjusts the prosthesis. At Dorset, fitting and rehabilitation could happen together, with both clinicians available to assess the same problem. NHS services can also bring these professions together, although arrangements vary between centres.
      </p>
      <p>
        She described that partnership during rehabilitation and component trials, including trials of microprocessor feet. If someone struggled to walk comfortably, she could assess their movement while the prosthetist checked the socket, alignment, or device settings. They could then see whether an adjustment helped. The patient did not have to carry an explanation from one appointment with their prosthetist to another to another with their physiotherapist, while each clinician tried to work out what the other had seen.
      </p>
      <p>
        <This in-house partnership between a prosthetist and physical therapist is not common in the US. As a result, there can be a significant disconnect that hinders efffective rehabilitation. I had seen patients spend weeks struggling with discomfort or difficulty putting on a prosthesis because the problem had not reached the person who could address it. I had seen some collaboration between clinics and independent physical therapists, but never together in the same building. When I later took the Tube to Amersham, I wanted to see that partnership in practice.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>What Having a Workshop Changes</h2>
      <p>
        The first thing that surprised me was the size of the building. The clinic where I had interned in Kentucky had two main spaces: a consultation room and a workshop. The prosthetist made plaster models and assembled and adjusted limbs in the workshop, but sent models to central fabrication facilities in Ohio and Tennessee for the check and definitive sockets. That arrangement kept the workshop smaller, while adding shipping time between stages.
      </p>
      <p>
        At Amersham, both check sockets and definitive sockets were fabricated in-house. Alongside consultation rooms and a physiotherapy gym, the clinic had spaces for plaster work, model rectification, and socket fabrication. Rectification is the process of modifying a model to guide the socket’s fit. Seeing the workspaces helped me understand why incorporating fabrication into a clinic changes both its footprint and the way people work within it.
      </p>
    <figure class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
      <img src="/assets/img/keegan/united-kingdom/amersham-plaster-model-workbench.webp"

        <span class="itinerary-story__source">Photo: author, Dorset Orthopaedic, Amersham.</span>
      </figcaption>

      <p>
        A prosthetist talked me through the fitting process. Like the clinic in Kentucky, the team might work through two or three transparent check sockets while refining a patient’s fit. These temporary sockets allow clinicians to assess contact and comfort before making a definitive version. He explained that patients using them outside the clinic needed close review, often weekly, because durability can vary depending on the person’s weight and how they use the limb. In some cases, the team reinforced a check socket with carbon fiber. I had seen fiberglass reinforcement before, but carbon fiber reinforcement was new to me.
      </p>
      <p>
        With a workshop in-house, I was curious if the team could cast someone in the morning and give them their check socket that afternoon. This way, the person could be cast and start walking on a temporary socket all in one day, as opposed scheduling a follow up to receive their socket. The prosthetist said it was possible, although unusual and generally reserved for urgent circumstances. It was not the normal timetable, but having that option can matter a lot.
      </p>
      <p>
        In Kentucky, I had met patients traveling more than three hours each way for care. Consultation, casting, fitting, and adjustments could mean repeated journeys before a limb was ready for regular use. An appointment’s cost often includes time away from work, transport, and the effort of getting there. Combining stages cannot remove the need to check fit carefully, but it can reduce part of the burden. I began to think of fabrication capacity as an access issue as well as a manufacturing decision.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>Who Can Reach This Kind of Care?</h2>
      <p>
        Seeing the workshop and rehabilitation spaces made the difference in funding tangible. A clinic may have the equipment and expertise to offer extensive care, but a patient still needs a way to pay for it. For someone with a personal injury claim, a case manager may help coordinate that process. Dorset’s <a href="https://www.dorset-ortho.com/offer/information-for-defendant-insurers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">published referral pathway</a> describes clinical assessment, recommendations, reporting to insurers, and follow-up. This is a different route from relying on an ordinary private medical insurance policy to cover a prosthesis.
      </p>
      <p>
        The team described assessment as a way to understand the person’s life before deciding what services to provide. Work, hobbies, confidence, and the effects of the injury could all shape a plan, including recommendations for physiotherapy, occupational therapy, or psychological support. My understanding was that the clinic and case manager stay in communication as needs change. Receiving a leg is one stage in that process, though it does not necessarily mark the end of rehabilitation.
      </p>
      <p>
        I asked whether maintaining relationships with established patients eventually overwhelmed a clinic that also accepted new referrals. The prosthetist described a changing rhythm of care consisting of frequent appointments during early fitting and rehabilitation, followed by more widely spaced reviews once someone was comfortable using their prosthesis. People might also choose a clinic because of an established relationship rather than simply its distance from home. Continuity had value, but it did not require the same intensity of care at every stage.
      </p>
      </div>

      <h2>Watching the Conversation Become Care</h2>
      <p>
        Back downstairs, I met a patient who had lost his leg above the knee in a bus accident. He had initially received care through the NHS and later moved to Dorset, where he was working with the physiotherapist.
      </p>
      <p>
        I did not establish why he changed services. One possibility I wondered about was the timing of funding: someone might begin NHS rehabilitation while exploring whether private care could be financed. That was a question prompted by his experience, not an explanation he gave me. What I did know was that his care had crossed between the two settings, making the distinction less tidy than separate descriptions of “NHS patients” and “private patients” might suggest.
      </p>
      <p>
        He told me he had moved from a Blatchford Orion3 knee to an Ottobock C-Leg. I asked whether changing between two microprocessor knees had required much adjustment, and he confirmed that there had been a learning curve. It was a useful reminder that being familiar with one device does not make another immediately intuitive. Learning how a knee responds is part of learning to trust it.
      </p>
      <p>
        He also described difficulties with an earlier pin-lock arrangement and the space it required in his prosthetic setup. I did not have enough information to identify the cause of the problems he experienced. Still, it brought attention to something easy to miss when looking at a component on its own: the suspension system, socket, knee, and foot all have to fit within the space available and work together.
      </p>

      <p>
        While the physiotherapist led exercises, a prosthetist was making adjustments to the patient’s leg. When she returned with it, he put it on and began walking with both clinicians watching. The physiotherapist moved in front of and behind him, offering cues about his movement. She referred back to the muscles they had just worked on and encouraged him to bring that awareness into his steps. At the same time, the prosthetist asked how the knee’s resistance felt and considered its response during different tasks.
      </p>
      <p>
        Here was the arrangement the physiotherapist had described over Teams, unfolding in front of me. If he found a movement difficult, the people addressing the device and the movement were watching the same attempt. They could hear his feedback, adjust their approach, and see what changed. There was less room for an observation to be lost between appointments.
      </p>
      <p>
        The prosthetist also helped him use the knee’s phone app to explore activity settings. Ottobock describes <a href="https://www.ottobock.com/en-au/mymodes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">MyModes</a> as additional settings configured with a clinician, which users can switch between through the Cockpit app or prescribed movement patterns. During this session, the team adapted an activity setting for a step exercise. I did not record the exact configuration reliably enough to explain its mechanics, but I could see the purpose: giving him support while he practiced the movement the physiotherapist was teaching.
      </p>
      <p>
        The technology and rehabilitation were being adjusted together. The prosthetist helped make the knee’s behavior understandable, and the physiotherapist connected that behavior to an exercise. Another comment from the visit stayed with me: discomfort was something to bring to the team, rather than simply push through. That makes particular sense in a setting where several people can help work out what the discomfort is telling them.
      </p>

      <div>
      <h2>What I Took Away</h2>
      <p>
        In Iceland, I had been drawn to the coordination of sensors, software, and mechanical parts. Amersham showed me another kind of coordination: a socket being adjusted while rehabilitation continued, a clinician explaining a setting, and a patient testing whether it helped. The device remained central, but its usefulness depended on these exchanges.
      </p>
      <p>
        The physiotherapist also pointed toward changes she hoped to see, including developments in osseointegration, digital methods, and devices that are less bulky or demanding to charge. Those conversations will be worth returning to. For now, the most convincing example of progress was already happening in the room: the right people could respond to the same problem together.
      </p>

      <h2>Who Decides How a Body Is Seen?</h2>
      <p>
        At the V&amp;A, I came across several self-portraits by Japanese multimedia artist Mari Katayama, from her series titled <em>tree of life</em>. As a photographer mzself, I was fixated on ____ .
      </p>
      <p>
        The series grew from the artist’s interest in how images can be reproduced and reshaped, and how other people’s expectations shape our relationship with our bodies. She built the mirrored setting in her studio and photographed herself, taking control of both sides of the camera. As <a href="https://www.yutakakikutakegallery.com/exhibitions/tree-of-life/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">her gallery’s account of the work</a> explains, that choice also explores the power a photographer holds over the person being photographed.
      </p>

      <div class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
      <p>
        Much of my fellowship has involved asking how a prosthesis helps someone navigate daily life. These images prompted a different question: how much room do we leave for that person to decide how they are seen? The artist’s photographs invite attention without offering a simple story about limb loss. For me, their impact lies in that refusal to make a body immediately explainable.
      </p>

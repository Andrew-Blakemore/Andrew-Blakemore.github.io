    <h2>From Iceland, Around the World</h2>
<p class="itinerary-story__dek">
For such a small country, Iceland has played a surprisingly large role in modern prosthetics. It is home to Össur, one of the largest prosthetic manufacturers in the world. Founded in 1971 by Icelandic prosthetist Össur Kristinsson, the company grew from his early work with silicone liners into a global manufacturer of prosthetic knees, feet, liners, and other components.
  </p>
<p class="itinerary-story__dek">
That made Reykjavík an obvious stop for this project. I had already spent time looking at how prostheses are fabricated and fitted; at Össur, I could follow the process farther upstream and ask how the components themselves are designed, tested, and manufactured before they ever reach a clinic.
  </p>

      <h2>The Prosthetics Landscape</h2>
      <p>
        I started the visit talking with a product design engineer in Össur's R&amp;D team who has worked extensively on Navii, the company's newest microprocessor knee (MPK). His background is in mechanical engineering, and much of his work has centered on the actuator inside the knee. I was interested in the hardware, of course, but the question I kept coming back to was much less mechanical: when an engineer in Reykjavík is designing a knee that may eventually be worn by people all over the world, how does he know what those people actually need?
      </p>

<br>

      <p>
        One answer is that the engineers are not working in isolation. Feedback comes back through prosthetists who fit and troubleshoot the products, and that feedback can make its way into firmware updates or future designs. He gave me an example involving clinicians in Germany whose feedback helped shape an update to Navii's behavior on inclines. Össur also works with highly experienced prosthetic users who put products through conditions that are difficult to recreate in a lab. That distinction stuck with me. A test rig can tell you whether a component survives a certain load; a person can tell you whether it actually works when life gets messy.
      </p>

      <p>
        That becomes especially important once a product is already on the market. Engineering does not stop when a knee is released. Problems surface, users find edge cases, clinicians notice patterns, and the next version begins taking shape. Much of the work he described was less about inventing something completely new and more about making an existing idea last longer, behave better, or fail less often.
      </p>

      <p>
        We ended up moving from there into manufacturing. I have been paying close attention to additive manufacturing because 3D printing shows up so often in conversations about the future of prosthetics, particularly in upper-limb devices. At Össur, though, the picture was more complicated. 3D printing is extremely useful in R&amp;D because an engineer can make a prototype, check its geometry or fit, change the design, and try again without committing to expensive production tooling. That does not automatically make it the best way to manufacture thousands of finished parts. For high-volume products, molding, machining, composites, and other established processes can still make far more sense.
      </p>

<br>

      <p>
        The part of the conversation that surprised me most had almost nothing to do with mechanics. I asked how much healthcare systems influence product design, expecting the answer to stay fairly high-level. Instead, we ended up talking about the United States and reimbursement. American prosthetic care relies heavily on billing codes and insurance approval, so a component can be technically appropriate and still be difficult for a patient to obtain. I had thought of that mostly as a problem faced by prosthetists and patients. Hearing it discussed from the manufacturer's side made me realize how far upstream those rules can reach.
      </p>

      <p>
        We talked about the Navii alongside the RHEO KNEE, an earlier Össur MPK that also uses magnetorheological technology. I had initially thought about a newer knee in the same way I might think about a new phone: the new model arrives and eventually replaces the old one. Prosthetics do not work that neatly. Different products can remain useful for different patients, and coverage rules can influence which options are realistically available. It was another reminder that "better" is a slippery word in prosthetics. Better for whom? Better under which insurance policy? Better for what kind of daily life?
      </p>

      <p>
        That led into a harder issue: reimbursement can create incentives of its own. Different components may be reimbursed differently, which creates at least the possibility that financial considerations pull against the question clinicians are supposed to be asking: what is the best option for this patient? I do not want to suggest that this describes prosthetists generally; it does not. What interested me was that an engineer on the manufacturing side was familiar with the tension at all. I had been treating reimbursement as something that happened near the end of the chain. It clearly reaches much farther back.
      </p>

      <p>
        We also talked about something much more visible: whether people want a prosthesis to blend in or stand out. In the United States and Europe, there seems to be growing room for prostheses to be treated as something worth showing. Sockets are decorated, covers are offered in different colors, and the device can become part of how someone presents themselves rather than something that has to imitate a biological leg.
      </p>

      <p>
        Then he mentioned a very different experience that had been described to him by a prosthetic user in South Africa: concern that an expensive prosthetic leg could attract theft. I cannot take one person's experience and turn it into a statement about an entire country, but the contrast stayed with me. In one setting, making a prosthesis more visible can be an act of confidence or self-expression. In another, visibility may feel like a risk. The exact same piece of technology can carry a completely different meaning depending on where it is worn.
      </p>

      <p>
        That matters for my project too. I have wanted to speak with people using sophisticated MPKs in places where heat, dust, water, long distances, or limited access to service can put very different demands on a device. But the conversation made me realize that finding those users is not only a technical or logistical problem. Whether someone is comfortable showing or even discussing an expensive prosthesis can depend on the environment around them.
      </p>

      <p>
        Our conversation eventually reached an even larger question: who should have access to prosthetic technology during war? We discussed Össur's decision to suspend sales to Russia after the invasion of Ukraine and the broader tension between corporate withdrawal and continued access to medical devices. I left without a clean answer. A prosthesis is a commercial product, but for the person wearing it, it may also be the difference between walking independently and not. How should a company weigh those two realities when the country it is selling into is engaged in a war? And how do you impose consequences on a state without treating an individual amputee as though he or she is responsible for that state's actions? It is one of those questions that became harder, not easier, the longer I thought about it.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div>
      <h2>Inside the Lab</h2>
      <p>
        After we finished talking, we headed upstairs. This was the part of the visit where ideas I had been hearing about suddenly became physical. Finished prosthetic knees are compact, sealed objects; there is not much from the outside to suggest what is happening inside them. On the benches in the lab, that neat exterior disappeared. I could see actuator parts, electronics, test fixtures, partially assembled feet, and components at different stages of production.
      </p>

      <p>
        Navii was the piece I was most curious about. Unlike many MPKs that regulate knee resistance hydraulically, Navii uses magnetorheological (MR) fluid. The fluid contains tiny magnetic particles. Apply a magnetic field and its resistance to shear changes almost immediately; change the field and the resistance changes with it. The knee's controller can use that effect to alter how strongly the joint resists rotation as the user moves through the gait cycle. It is a strange idea to see in person: part of what controls a person's knee is a fluid whose mechanical behavior can be changed electronically in a fraction of a second.
      </p>

      <p>
        Seeing the actuator broken down also corrected one of my own assumptions. I had been picturing the sensors as somehow creating the magnetic effect themselves. They do not. Sensors tell the controller what is happening at the leg; the controller then changes the magnetic field in the actuator. That distinction sounds small, but seeing the pieces separately made the whole feedback loop much easier to understand: sense what the user is doing, decide how the knee should respond, then change the resistance at the joint.
      </p>

      <p>
        The lab was not just about knees. I also spent time around the PROPRIO FOOT, a microprocessor-controlled prosthetic foot with an actively adjusting ankle. A conventional prosthetic foot cannot simply dorsiflex on command the way an anatomical ankle can. PROPRIO can change its ankle angle, including lifting the toe during swing and adapting its position for slopes or different heel heights. Those adjustments sound subtle until you think about how often a toe only needs to miss the ground by a few millimeters for the difference to matter.
      </p>

      <p>
        Nearby were components from the Power Knee as well. That comparison helped me separate technologies that are easy to lump together under the word "bionic." Navii primarily controls resistance: it decides how freely or firmly the knee should move. Power Knee can actively add power through a motor. PROPRIO FOOT moves the ankle. All three use electronics and sensors, but they are solving different mechanical problems.
      </p>

      <p>
        From there we moved toward the manufacturing side of the building. The atmosphere changed quickly: less laboratory bench, more open production floor, with machining, cutting, pressing, assembly, and inspection happening around us. I had spent a lot of the conversation thinking about innovation, but the manufacturing floor kept pulling me toward a less glamorous question: how do you make the same complicated thing correctly over and over again?
      </p>

<br>

      <p>
        I heard terms such as kitting, actuator filling, pressing, final assembly, verification, and testing as we moved through the space. None sounds as exciting as a new MPK, but that may be the point. A prosthetic component can be clever on a computer screen and still be useless if it cannot be manufactured consistently, survive repeated loading, keep water out, or behave the same way thousands of cycles later. For a device that someone may trust with every step, reliability is not the boring part of the engineering. It is the engineering.
      </p>

      <p>
        That was probably my biggest takeaway from Össur. I arrived wanting to see advanced prosthetic technology, and I did. What interested me more by the end was everything wrapped around it: feedback from users, decisions made by clinicians, reimbursement rules in another country, manufacturing constraints, cultural attitudes, and the repetitive work required to make a sophisticated component dependable. The farther I follow a prosthesis backward from the person wearing it, the harder it becomes to point to any one place and say, "this is where the design happens."
      </p>

    <h2>Following a Lead in Turkey</h2>
    <p class="itinerary-story__dek">I can’t recall exactly how the discussion went down. A friend of a friend mentioned an opportunity they knew of, and somewhere between that conversation and my arrival in Turkey, I signed myself up for an olive harvest. I typically avoid rolling the dice like that when it comes to living accommodations, but the experience was far too intriguing to pass up. That is how I ended up spending my first two weeks in the country working on a farm on the Datça peninsula.</p>

      <h2 id="knidia">An Olive Farm in Southwestern Turkey</h2>
      <p>Getting to the farm took a few more connections than the friend-of-a-friend variety. After my time in Poland, I made my way back to Berlin and flew to Dalaman, on Turkey’s southwestern coast. From there, I took a coach to Marmaris, then a dolmuş, a shared minibus, to Datça. The farm was still another 30 kilometers or so to the west-northwest. I caught a lift to the closest village, then trekked the rest of the way myself. As I neared my destination, I was greeted by orderly rows of grapevines and scattered olive trees.</p>
      <figure class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
        <img src="{{ '/assets/img/keegan/turkey/vineyard-at-sunset.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Rows of grapevines beneath a sunset sky, with wooded hills surrounding the farm." width="1536" height="1152" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
        <figcaption>Sunset over the vineyard. A pretty good place to end a long day of travel.</figcaption>
      </figure>
      <br>
      <p>The ecofarm was tucked into a valley, with vineyards, fruit trees, and bungalows embedded into a working farm. It grew grapes, olives, almonds, lemons, and mandarins commercially, alongside a sprawling kitchen garden full of tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, and herbs.</p>
      <p>My main job was bringing in the harvest. My team spread large sheets beneath the trees, then worked through the branches with the zeytin hasat makineleri, shaking and combing the olives loose so they fell onto the fabric below. From there, we gathered them up and moved on to the next tree. The idea was simple enough, but the work quickly turned grueling as the terrain shifted from a flat, sparse field to steep, overgrown terrace plots.</p>

      <p>But this was only part of the job. Throughout the harvest, we also processed the olives into oil, and I helped assemble, operate, and clean the machinery.</p>
      <figure class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
        <video controls playsinline preload="metadata" aria-label="Olives moving through the farm’s oil-processing equipment" style="display: block; width: 100%; max-height: 620px; border-radius: 18px; background: #18201a;">

      <br>
      <p>During processing, I learned more about how the timing of the harvest shapes the oil. Olives picked early, while still green, tend to produce a bold, grassy oil with some bitterness and a peppery kick. They generally yield less oil, but it is often richer in the natural antioxidant compounds that help preserve its freshness. As olives ripen, turning purple and eventually almost black, they yield more oil with a milder, rounder flavor, sometimes with buttery notes.</p>
      <figure class="itinerary-story__body-copy">
        <img src="{{ '/assets/img/keegan/turkey/olives-in-hand.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Three green, mottled purple, and dark olives resting in an open hand above a bin of harvested olives." width="1536" height="1152" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
        <figcaption>Because our harvest started late, the olives we gathered were a mix of green, black, and every shade in between.</figcaption>
      </figure>
      <br>
      <p>Once the harvest ended, I spent a few days pruning the olive trees, clearing away some of the lower growth. After all the gathering and processing, this was a satisfying change of pace, almost cathartic. There was something nice about being able to stand back and see the difference in a single tree.</p>

      <p>Two weeks hardly made me an olive farmer, but they gave me a much more personal connection to something I had known very little about. I'm grateful I stayed long enough to take part in multiple facets of the experience, from bringing down the fruit to working with the processing equipment and tending the trees afterward.</p>

    <section class="itinerary-story__section" aria-labelledby="istanbul">
      <h2 id="istanbul">From Olive Groves to the Big City</h2>
      <p>After leaving the farm, some of the other workers, from Moscow, Russia, and Arizona, USA, and I headed to Istanbul for a few days. It's funny how a couple of weeks of demanding physical labor can bring the unlikeliest of acquaintances together.</p>
      <figure class="itinerary-story__body-copy" style="max-width: 465px;">
        <img src="{{ '/assets/img/keegan/turkey/galata-tower.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Two fellow farm workers and me posing by the Galata Tower" width="1152" height="1536" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style="height: auto; max-height: none;">
        <figcaption>Taken on the European side of Istanbul</figcaption>
      </figure>
      <br>
      <p>I would be remiss if I didn't mention our side quest to IKEA. Our friend from Russia had never been (IKEA permanently withdrew from Russia following the country's invasion of Ukraine in 2022), so we were honor-bound to introduce him to the Labyrinth. Suffice it to say, he left with many souvenirs, and we all left with bellies full of meatballs.</p>
      <figure class="itinerary-story__body-copy" style="max-width: 465px;">
        <img src="{{ '/assets/img/keegan/turkey/ikea-trip.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Two fellow farm workers posing by IKEA" width="1152" height="1536" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style="height: auto; max-height: none;">
        <figcaption>A sight for sore eyes</figcaption>
      </figure>
      <br>
      <p>I’m still not entirely sure how a friend-of-a-friend introduction led me from Poland to an olive grove and then to showing someone around IKEA in Istanbul, but I’m glad I followed through with it. Some parts of this trip take a great deal of planning. Others do not. Through my travels, I’m learning to leave a little more room for the unexpected and say yes to the strange, spur-of-the-moment opportunities that might become some of my favorite memories.</p>
    </section>
